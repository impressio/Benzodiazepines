Molecular & clinical pharmacology

Benzodiazepines & the GABAA receptor

Benzodiazepines (BZDs) are positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) of the GABAA receptor. They bind at the extracellular α+/γ2− interface, distinct from the two orthosteric GABA sites at the β+/α− interfaces, and increase the apparent affinity of GABA, thereby increasing the frequency of Cl− channel opening without gating the channel in the absence of agonist. This resource integrates receptor structure, subunit pharmacology, drug properties and pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic models.

Receptor subunits & clinical effects

The GABAA receptor family comprises 19 subunits (α1-6, β1-3, γ1-3, δ, ε, θ, π, ρ1-3); the predominant synaptic isoform is α1β2γ2 with a 2α:2β:1γ stoichiometry. High-affinity BZD binding requires a γ2 subunit and an α1, α2, α3 or α5 subunit bearing a conserved histidine (α1 His101, rat numbering). Point-mutation studies in mice attribute sedation and anterograde amnesia mainly to α1-, anxiolysis to α2/α3-, and aspects of learning and tolerance to α5-containing receptors. The cards below summarise the six α isoforms, with fractional BZD-site occupancy for the ligand and free concentration selected in Allosteric modulation at the receptor (kept in sync with Pharmacodynamics), and the β, γ2 and δ subunits; selecting an α card sets the isoform for all simulations.

5

Pharmacodynamics

Allosteric model with competitive binding at the BZD site: the fractional BZD-site occupancy is θ = (C/Ki) / (1 + C/Ki + Cflu/Kflu) and the apparent GABA EC50 is EC50app = EC50 / [1 + (β − 1)·θ], where C is the free ligand concentration and Ki its dissociation constant, Cflu and Kflu those of flumazenil, and β the cooperativity factor of the ligand (β > 1, PAM; β = 1, neutral antagonist; β < 1, inverse agonist/NAM). A pure PAM shifts the GABA concentration-response curve to the left without altering Emax.

Pharmacokinetics

Biotransformation & active metabolites

Most BZDs undergo hepatic phase I oxidation (CYP3A4, CYP2C19) before phase II glucuronidation, often yielding pharmacologically active metabolites with long half-lives; lorazepam, oxazepam and temazepam are glucuronidated directly. These pathways determine the modifier effects simulated above.

Notes & limitations
  • Parameter values. Pharmacodynamic parameters (Ki, cooperativity factor β) and population pharmacokinetic parameters (t½, Vd, F, ka) are taken from FDA prescribing information, the IUPHAR/BPS Guide to Pharmacology, ChEMBL and primary papers, rounded for teaching; reference concentration ranges are indicative only. The source and verification status of each value are listed in the data tables.
  • Pharmacodynamic model. A single allosteric term shifts the apparent GABA EC50 in proportion to BZD-site occupancy. Receptor reserve, desensitisation, state-dependent binding and differences between synaptic and extrasynaptic populations are not represented.
  • Subtype selectivity shows affinity only. The selectivity radar plots binding affinity (pKi) per α isoform, without separate potency (pEC50) or efficacy plots. In this model the allosteric shift scales linearly with occupancy and each ligand has one cooperativity factor β for all isoforms, so potency follows pKi within about 0.2 log units and efficacy is uniform; such plots would duplicate the affinity profile. For the clinical agents included, this matches their pharmacology: classical BZDs are non-selective in efficacy, and zolpidem's α1 preference is one of affinity. Efficacy selectivity - compounds such as L-838,417 or TPA023 that bind α1 without potentiating it - is real but outside the scope of this model.
  • Pharmacokinetic model. One compartment with first-order absorption and elimination. Distribution kinetics are therefore absent: the single-dose effect of lipophilic agents such as diazepam is in reality shortened by redistribution from brain to peripheral tissue, which this model does not capture. For the same reason the early peak of such drugs is underestimated (about 3-fold for oral diazepam). Absorption rate constants were fitted to the time to peak reported in FDA labels or primary studies, and the model was then compared with independent single-dose studies: predicted peak concentrations were within 2-fold of observed values in most cases (see the validation in the data tables). The onset of action and Tmax shown in the administration-route card are clinical values.
  • Free concentration. Derived as peak plasma concentration × unbound fraction, assuming free brain concentration approximates free plasma concentration and ignoring active transport and protein-binding displacement. Occupancy uses the parent drug only: active metabolites (e.g. nordiazepam after diazepam) are not included, so occupancies at clinically equivalent doses are not directly comparable between drugs.
  • Combined exposure. In the plasma profile, parent drug and active metabolites are summed as concentrations without adjustment for differing potency.
  • Dose-response curves. Illustrative: GABA tone 5 µM at α1-containing receptors, with 1× dose set to ≈ 30% occupancy. Clinical effect bands and the opioid interaction term are approximate and chosen for teaching, not fitted to data.
  • Regional distribution. Abundance levels are a qualitative summary of immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridisation, mainly in rodent brain; human distribution is broadly similar but less completely mapped. Levels are relative within each isoform and not comparable in absolute receptor density between isoforms. Each structure is shaded uniformly, so laminar and nuclear differences (e.g. α3 confined to the reticular thalamic nucleus) appear only in the descriptions. The 3D brain combines the fsaverage cortical surface with structures segmented from the MNI152 2009c template, aligned by bounding boxes (accurate to a few millimetres); the hypothalamus, olfactory bulbs and spinal cord are approximated by simple shapes, and the cerebellar folia are rendered, not modelled. The 2D map is a lateral view rendered offline from the same model and coloured in the page, so it does not require WebGL; it is shown automatically if the 3D view is unavailable.
  • Channel gating and Cl⁻ flux. The 3D view animates a single receptor with the kinetic (Markov) scheme of α1β3γ2L receptors of Haas and Macdonald (1999; rate constants as corrected in the 2004 erratum): two GABA binding steps, three open states with bursts of brief closures, and fast, intermediate and slow desensitised states. In the animation GABA is applied at the selected concentration in repeating cycles of application and washout (timing shown under the gate state), so activation, desensitisation, deactivation and recovery recur; the time course of a 4 s application and the recovery after washout are plotted below the viewer. Animation time is slowed 20-fold, and the gate is drawn open for each burst of openings (the brief closures within a burst are not drawn). A BZD-site ligand multiplies the GABA association rate at both binding steps by the EC50 shift of the allosteric model, so openings become more frequent while open and burst durations stay unchanged (Rogers et al., 1994; Lavoie and Twyman, 1996). Other studies place the BZD effect on a preactivation step after GABA binding (Gielen et al., 2012), and the slower deactivation of IPSCs seen in neurons (Mellor and Randall, 1997) is not reproduced by this mechanism. The scheme was measured with GABA alone, not with any BZD-site ligand: every ligand uses the same scheme and differs only through its occupancy and cooperativity factor, which set its EC50 shift; drug-specific kinetic effects are not modelled. The scheme was derived from recombinant receptors at 22-23 °C and is used for all α isoforms; the open probability readouts use this scheme, whereas the concentration-response and dose-effect curves use the equilibrium allosteric model, which has no desensitisation. The simulated IPSC in Pharmacodynamics has the rise and biexponential decay of IPSCs in cultured hippocampal neurons (Jones and Westbrook, 1995), with decay prolonged by PAMs as calibrated to Mellor and Randall (1997). Single-channel currents follow the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equations: the Cl⁻ permeability follows from γ = 27 pS in symmetrical 140 mM Cl⁻, and HCO₃⁻ permeates with PHCO3/PCl = 0.18 (HCO₃⁻ 25 mM outside, 15 mM inside). Extracellular Cl⁻ is 130 mM; intracellular Cl⁻ is 8 mM in a mature neuron (ECl = −74.5 mV, EGABA = −67.7 mV) or 30 mM in an immature neuron, where Cl⁻ uptake by NKCC1 before KCC2 expression keeps it high and GABA depolarises. Vm is adjustable (default −65 mV; 37 °C). In the mature gradient the Cl⁻ current rectifies outwardly (Bormann et al., 1987), so at the default Vm an open channel carries 0.047 pA of Cl⁻ current, far less than γ(Vm − ECl) predicts. The net Cl⁻ flux readout is Po × iCl/e at the peak and at steady state of the kinetic scheme. In the animation, ions reach the gate from the side the net flux comes from, at a rate proportional to it (none at ECl, where the unidirectional fluxes balance); they pass only while the gate is open and otherwise return, without queuing, so the mean flux is proportional to the open probability. Only net flux is drawn, and counts are scaled for display (scale shown under the flux readout). Membrane potential and ion concentrations are held constant: the voltage change caused by the current, Cl⁻ accumulation and saturation of permeation are not modelled. Flumazenil returns the flux to the GABA-alone level; it does not block the channel.
  • Structure. The displayed structure is the α1β3γ2 template (PDB 6HUP); other α isoforms are indicated by colour and labels only, and sequence differences are not modelled. Ligands other than diazepam and flumazenil are shown on the diazepam pose.
